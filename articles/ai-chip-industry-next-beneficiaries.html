The AI Chip Industry: Who Stands to Benefit Next?

NVIDIA, AMD, Micron, and SK Hynix all surged on Nov. 23, 2026. The money flowing into data centers — $208.7 billion in a single quarter — decides who benefits next.

By Wall Street Moves · November 24, 2026 · Last updated: November 24, 2026

Nov. 23, 2026 was a reminder that the AI chip trade moves as a group. NVIDIA closed at about $165, up just over 31%, after its board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive. Micron gained 19.04% to close at $707.70. SK Hynix gained 20.00% to close at $124.28. AMD gained 6.85% to close at $453.46. The iShares Semiconductor ETF jumped 13.63%.

Three days earlier, the same names had led the market down. On Nov. 20, NVIDIA fell 14.01% to $125.53, AMD fell 13.25% to $424.39, Micron fell 13.89% to $594.51, SK Hynix fell 13.11% to $103.57, and Broadcom closed at $304.41. The swing from Nov. 20 to Nov. 23 was not a change in the demand outlook. It was a change in the leadership outlook at one company, and the whole sector moved with it.

The demand engine is spending

The reason chip stocks move together is that they share the same customer: the data-center buildout. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta spent a combined $208.7 billion on capital expenditures in the third quarter of 2026, up 84% from $113.4 billion in the third quarter of 2025. That total mixes company-reported measures: Amazon's figure is gross purchases of property and equipment, Alphabet's is cash purchases, Microsoft's includes finance leases and Meta's includes finance-lease principal. On a pure cash basis, the four spent about $202 billion, up about 108% from $97.3 billion a year earlier. Their 2026 spending guidance adds up to roughly $720 billion to $740 billion. Amazon and Alphabet have said explicitly they expect to spend more in 2027; Microsoft said its fiscal 2027 capital spending will grow year over year, and Meta has given no 2027 direction.

NVIDIA still sets the pace

NVIDIA remains the center of the AI chip market because it sells the most complete system: accelerators, networking, and the software that ties them together. For the fiscal third quarter ended Oct. 25, 2026, the company reported data center revenue of $107.6 billion, up 110% from a year earlier, on total revenue of $115.8 billion. It forecast fourth-quarter revenue of $130 billion, and said its Vera Rubin platform has shipped. With Huang back as chief executive, the product cycle he built — Rubin is the next platform after Blackwell — has its architect back in charge.

Analysts moved quickly. Bernstein upgraded NVIDIA to Outperform on Nov. 23 and kept a $200 price target, after cutting the target from $400 to $200 on Nov. 12. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives kept a $225 price target and an Outperform rating, while arguing the stock is worth $300 with Huang and $225 without him. The gap between those two numbers is one measure of how much of NVIDIA's value investors attach to a single person.

The second tier: AMD and the memory makers

AMD is the clearest second beneficiary if data-center customers want a second supplier of AI accelerators. AMD's shares fell further than NVIDIA's in percentage terms during the Nov. 20 selloff and recovered less on Nov. 23 — up 6.85% against NVIDIA's roughly 31.5% — which says the market still treats AMD as a participant in the buildout rather than its driver. The company's opportunity is to convert that participation into a larger share of accelerator spending as customers diversify supply.

The memory makers may be the most direct read on how much hardware the buildout actually needs. Every AI server needs high-bandwidth memory alongside its accelerators, and investors bid Micron and SK Hynix up about 20% each on Nov. 23. The two companies' combined move was larger than AMD's and second only to NVIDIA's, which suggests the market expects memory demand to stay tight as data-center construction continues.

The risks are shared too

The same forces that lift the group can cut it. Reuters reported on Nov. 24 that analysts and investors have grown concerned about circular AI financing deals, in which chipmakers help customers fund purchases of their chips and the construction of data centers. NVIDIA has backed AI companies and cloud providers and provided guarantees connected to data-center projects that use its equipment. Critics say such arrangements can make it harder to assess the quality of chipmakers' revenue and their true financial exposure.

Export controls are a second shared risk. The U.S. government restricts sales of advanced AI chips to China, and NVIDIA's fourth-quarter guidance assumes no data-center compute sales to Chinese customers. The political scrutiny around Huang's return — including a scheduled closed-door interview with House committees on Dec. 1 and a public Senate hearing on Dec. 3 — keeps that risk in view. A third risk is the spending itself: Alphabet reported negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion in the third quarter, and Meta reported negative free cash flow of $2.12 billion. If the buyers' cash flow stays under pressure, their spending plans can change faster than chipmakers' production plans.

Who benefits next

Three groups are positioned to benefit if the buildout continues at its current pace. First, NVIDIA, which captures the largest share of accelerator spending and now has its founder back. Second, the memory suppliers, Micron and SK Hynix, whose products are required in every AI server regardless of which accelerator it uses. Third, AMD and the custom-silicon designers, which benefit if large customers spread orders beyond a single supplier.

The order matters. On Nov. 23, the market ranked them exactly that way: NVIDIA first, memory second, AMD third. The ranking will change if the next set of data changes — hyperscaler capital spending in the fourth quarter, the ramp of the Rubin platform, and memory pricing are the figures to watch. Until then, the lesson of Nov. 20 and Nov. 23 stands: in the AI chip industry, the stocks rise and fall together, and the difference between a 31% day and a 14% day can be a single board vote.
