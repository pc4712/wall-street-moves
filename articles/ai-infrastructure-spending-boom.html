The AI Infrastructure Spending Boom: Opportunity or Risk?

Four companies spent $208.7 billion on capital projects in one quarter, and all four plan to spend more in 2027. The opportunity is real. So is the bill.

By Wall Street Moves · November 24, 2026 · Last updated: November 24, 2026

Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta spent a combined $208.7 billion on capital expenditures in the third quarter of 2026, up 84% from $113.4 billion in the third quarter of 2025. It was the largest quarterly total the four companies have ever reported. Their guidance for 2026 adds up to roughly $720 billion to $740 billion, and each company has said it expects to spend more again in 2027.

The scale is hard to overstate. The four companies' combined capital spending rose 84% from 2024 to 2025 alone, from $224 billion to $413 billion, and the pace quickened again in 2026. Add Oracle, which spent $28.5 billion in its quarter ended in August, up 235% from a year earlier, and the five companies spent $231.4 billion in their latest quarters — more than double the $106.3 billion they spent a year earlier.

The opportunity case

The spending is not speculative in the usual sense: it is tied to revenue that is already growing fast. Amazon Web Services reported revenue of $46.0 billion in the third quarter, up 39% from a year earlier. Google Cloud revenue more than doubled to $30.7 billion. Microsoft's Azure grew 48%. NVIDIA, which sells the accelerators that fill these data centers, reported data center revenue of $107.6 billion in its fiscal third quarter, up 110% from a year earlier.

The companies describe the spending as a response to demand they cannot yet meet. Amazon said it expects to spend "meaningfully more" in 2027. Alphabet's chief financial officer said spending would "increase significantly in 2027." Meta narrowed its 2026 range upward at the low end, to $135 billion to $145 billion from $130 billion to $145 billion, and said it expects to keep investing aggressively. Microsoft guided to more than $50 billion of capital spending in the December quarter alone.

Disclosed AI revenue is still a fraction of the spending — Microsoft's last disclosed AI revenue run-rate was over $37 billion, from March 2026, and Amazon Web Services' was over $25 billion, from June 2026 — but the direction is clear: the cloud businesses are growing at 39% to 48% a year, and the companies are spending to keep that growth going.

The risk case

The bill for the buildout is now arriving faster than the cash to pay for it. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on property and equipment in the third quarter against $42.3 billion of operating cash flow, producing negative free cash flow of $9.1 billion for the quarter. Meta's capital spending reached $34.9 billion, and its free cash flow came in at minus $2.12 billion. Amazon's trailing 12-month free cash flow swung to an outflow of $42.4 billion, from an inflow of $14.8 billion a year earlier. Microsoft is the exception: its $58.0 billion of operating cash flow in the September quarter covered its $39.4 billion of property and equipment additions with about $18.6 billion to spare.

The gap is being filled with debt. Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle issued $218.1 billion of index-eligible investment-grade bonds in 2026 by early October, more than double the $107.3 billion they issued in 2025, according to FTSE Russell. Alphabet has also disclosed $924 billion of future purchase commitments — contracts it has signed and must pay for whether or not demand holds up.

A second risk is harder to measure. Reuters reported on Nov. 24 that analysts and investors have grown concerned about circular AI financing deals, in which chipmakers help customers fund purchases of their chips and the construction of data centers. NVIDIA has backed AI companies and cloud providers and provided guarantees connected to data-center projects that use its equipment. Critics say such arrangements can make it harder for investors to assess the quality of chipmakers' revenue and their true financial exposure. An investigation into one company does not prove anything about the others, but the financing question applies to the whole buildout.

Opportunity or risk?

The honest answer is that it is both, and the balance depends on a single question: does revenue catch up with spending? The bull case is that cloud revenue is growing 39% to 48% a year, AI demand is still outrunning supply, and the companies spending the money are the most profitable in the world. The bear case is that free cash flow has turned negative at two of the four biggest spenders, debt issuance has doubled, and $924 billion of purchase commitments cannot be cancelled if demand slows.

History offers no clean parallel. The buildout resembles the railroad and telecom booms in one respect — enormous capital spending ahead of proven returns — and differs in another: the customers are already paying. Cloud revenue is real, growing, and large. The test is not whether the spending is justified on the current evidence, but whether the growth rate of that revenue stays ahead of the growth rate of the spending.

Four figures will answer that question in the coming quarters: fourth-quarter capital spending at each company, cloud revenue growth against the 39% to 48% pace of the third quarter, free cash flow at Alphabet and Meta, and any change in the 2027 spending plans. If revenue growth holds and cash flow recovers, the boom looks like an investment. If spending keeps rising while revenue growth slows, it starts to look like a bill. Until then, the market has made its own judgment: on Nov. 23, the day NVIDIA's founder returned, chip stocks posted one of their best days ever — and the companies that buy the chips kept spending.
