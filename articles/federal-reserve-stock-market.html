The Federal Reserve and the Stock Market: What Investors Should Watch

The Federal Reserve's next decision comes Dec. 9, 2026. The target rate is 3.75% to 4.00%, inflation is still running hot, and futures markets expect another increase.

By Wall Street Moves · November 24, 2026 · Last updated: November 24, 2026

The Federal Open Market Committee meets Dec. 8–9, 2026, and announces its decision at 2:00 p.m. Eastern on Dec. 9. The meeting includes a new Summary of Economic Projections, the Fed's quarterly set of forecasts for growth, unemployment, and inflation. It is the last scheduled Fed meeting of 2026, and it arrives two weeks after the stock market's sharpest rally in more than a year and a half.

The backdrop is unusual: the Fed raised its target rate in September, and the market is pricing in another increase in December. That combination — rising rates and rising stocks — is the tension investors need to understand.

Where rates stand

The federal funds target range is 3.75% to 4.00%. The Federal Open Market Committee raised the range by 25 basis points on Sept. 16, 2026, and the new range took effect Sept. 17. At its Oct. 27–28 meeting, the committee held the range unchanged, in a 10–2 vote; the two dissenters preferred a 25-basis-point increase at that meeting. The effective federal funds rate — the rate banks actually pay each other overnight — was 3.88% on Nov. 20, according to the Federal Reserve's H.15 release. The bank prime loan rate is 7.00%.

Fed funds futures, which reflect what traders expect the Fed to do, were pricing in a December increase as a near-certainty. As of Nov. 23, CME FedWatch put a 25-basis-point increase at 93.2%, with a 6.8% chance of no change; by Nov. 24 the 25-basis-point figure was still about 93%, but the balance had shifted from a hold to a small chance of a larger increase, Reuters reported. That is a strong consensus, but it is not a promise: futures pricing moves with every inflation report, and the November consumer price index is scheduled for release on Dec. 10, the day after the Fed decision.

Inflation is still running hot

The October consumer price index, released by the Bureau of Labor Statistics on Nov. 10, rose 0.4% in October on a seasonally adjusted basis, after rising 0.6% in September. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy, rose 0.3% in October — the third straight month at 0.3%. Shelter rose 0.3% and accounted for more than 30% of the monthly increase. Food prices rose 0.5% — the fastest increase since April, according to KPMG — and energy rose 0.7% after a 5.2% jump in September.

For most series, the Bureau of Labor Statistics could not publish 12-month percent changes for October, because it did not collect survey data for October 2025 during the lapse in appropriations that year. KPMG, interpolating from the available data, estimated that consumer prices rose 4.2% from a year earlier, after rising 3.7% in September. UBS estimated that core PCE — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — was tracking an increase of about 3.3% from a year earlier in October, up from 3.1%. The three-month annualized pace of core CPI rose to roughly 3.7%, according to UBS, from below 2.0% in August.

KPMG's conclusion was direct: "October inflation cinches December rate hike." UBS reached a similar view, writing that the report increases the risk of moving from gradual quarterly increases to sequential 25-basis-point increases, in December and January — though UBS's base case remains increases in December and March.

Why stocks care about the Fed

Interest rates matter to stocks through two channels. First, higher rates raise borrowing costs for companies and households, which can slow spending and earnings growth. Second, higher rates raise the return on safe assets, which makes future corporate earnings worth less in current dollars. Both channels usually push stock valuations down — which is why a market rally during a rate-increase cycle deserves a closer look.

The Nov. 23 rally is a case in point. The S&P 500 rose 4.73% and the Nasdaq rose 6.51% on a single piece of company news: NVIDIA reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive. Nothing about the Fed changed that day. The rally was driven by one company's leadership decision, not by a change in the rate outlook. That distinction matters, because a rally built on company news can fade if the rate outlook turns against it.

The same tension shows up in housing. The average 30-year mortgage rate was 7.28%, according to Freddie Mac data from Nov. 19. Higher rates have not stopped the AI infrastructure buildout, but they have kept pressure on rate-sensitive parts of the economy and the market.

What to watch on Dec. 9

Four things will matter when the Fed announces its decision. First, the decision itself: a 25-basis-point increase would take the target range to 4.00% to 4.25%. Second, the Summary of Economic Projections, including the "dot plot" of officials' rate forecasts — the market will read it for how many more increases officials expect in 2027. Third, the statement's language on inflation, which will show whether officials see the October data as a temporary pickup or a persistent problem. Fourth, the chair's press conference at 2:30 p.m. Eastern, where the tone often moves markets more than the decision itself.

One more date matters: Dec. 10, when the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the November consumer price index. If November inflation comes in hot, it would support the case for further increases in early 2027. If it cools, it would give the Fed room to pause.

The market has already made its bet — a December increase, with more to come. The data between now and Dec. 9 will decide whether that bet was right.
