What Is Driving the Latest U.S. Stock Market Rebound?

The S&P 500 rose 4.73% and the Nasdaq jumped 6.51% on Nov. 23, 2026, ending a 7-day losing streak. One company supplied most of the spark — but the rally was broader than one stock.

By Wall Street Moves · November 24, 2026 · Last updated: November 24, 2026

United States stocks staged a massive relief rally on Nov. 23, 2026. The S&P 500 closed at 7,237.17, up 4.73%, its biggest one-day gain in more than a year and a half — the best since April 9, 2025. The Nasdaq Composite closed at 24,089.56, up 6.51%, after opening 7.3% higher — the biggest opening gain on record, based on market-opening pricing data back to 1991. The Dow Jones Industrial Average added 3.01% to close at 49,595.31, and the Russell 2000 small-cap index rose 3.40% to 2,669.66.

The move snapped a 7-day losing streak, the longest since February 2020, and pulled both the S&P 500 and the Dow out of technical correction territory. The scale of the reversal is the story. The cause is narrower, and the question now is whether one very good day becomes a durable turn.

The trigger: NVIDIA's founder returns

The immediate catalyst was NVIDIA. Before the open on Nov. 23, the company's board announced it had unanimously voted to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive officer, and director, 11 days after the company announced his resignation on Nov. 12. NVIDIA shares closed at about $165, up just over 31% — the largest one-day gain since May 9, 2003, according to Dow Jones Market Data — and the company added roughly $950 billion in market value in a single session, the largest single-day gain on record for any U.S. company.

The effect spread across the chip sector. Micron gained 19.04% to close at $707.70, SK Hynix gained 20.00% to close at $124.28, and AMD gained 6.85% to close at $453.46. The iShares Semiconductor ETF jumped 13.63%. Technology was the day's best-performing sector, up 8.97%, while energy was the laggard, down 1.06%.

The selloff it reversed

To understand the rebound, it helps to remember what came before it. On Nov. 20, the S&P 500 fell 3.70% to close at 6,910.39, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% to 22,617.73, and the Dow fell 1.93% to 48,147.33. It was the seventh straight down day for the market. The S&P 500 had fallen more than 10% below its Oct. 6 record close of 7,818.93, which put the index in correction territory. The Cboe Volatility Index, or VIX, closed at 35.89 on Nov. 20 after touching 40.03 during the session, its highest close since April 2025.

The selling had a specific shape. NVIDIA fell 14.01% on Nov. 20 to close at $125.53, after reports that the Securities and Exchange Commission is examining the company's accounting practices. AMD fell 13.25% to $424.39, Micron fell 13.89% to $594.51, and SK Hynix fell 13.11% to $103.57. The same chip names that led the decline on Nov. 20 led the rebound on Nov. 23 — a sign that the two moves were, in large part, the same trade running in reverse.

Breadth was strong, with a caveat

One encouraging detail: the rally was not confined to a handful of mega-cap stocks. Within the S&P 500, more than four-fifths of stocks advanced on Nov. 23. The Russell 2000's 3.40% gain showed small caps participating, and industrials and consumer discretionary stocks also rose. The VIX fell about 26% to 26.62, a sharp drop in fear — although 26 is still above the 20 level that usually marks calm markets.

There is a caveat in the same data. The equal-weight S&P 500, which gives every stock the same influence instead of weighting by size, rose only 2.59%, well behind the 4.73% gain in the capitalization-weighted index, according to data compiled by CappNotes. That gap means the largest companies did most of the lifting. A durable recovery usually needs the average stock to participate, not just the largest ones, and market watchers will be looking for banks, industrials, small caps, and the equal-weight index to keep pace in the coming weeks.

One day is a data point, not a trend

The biggest one-day gains in market history have often clustered in bear markets rather than at the start of durable recoveries, and analysts were quick to say one day does not confirm a lasting turn. Real bottoms usually look quieter: a retest of the low on lighter selling, improving breadth across sectors, and a steady decline in volatility rather than a one-day collapse in the VIX.

Three tests will matter most in the sessions ahead. First, follow-through: do buyers return on the next down day, or does the rally fade at the first sign of selling? Second, broadening: does leadership spread beyond chips into financials, industrials, and small caps? Third, volatility: does the VIX keep falling toward 20, or does it stall near 26?

What to watch next

The calendar gives the market several events to react to. Jensen Huang is scheduled for a closed-door interview with House committees on Dec. 1 and a public Senate hearing on Dec. 3, and the investigations into NVIDIA remain open. The Federal Reserve meets Dec. 8–9, with its decision due Dec. 9, and the November consumer price index is scheduled for release on Dec. 10. Any of those can move the same stocks that moved on Nov. 23.

For now, the numbers say this: the market fell hard for 7 straight sessions, then posted its best day in more than a year and a half on a single piece of company news. The rebound is real, and it is broad. Whether it lasts depends on what happens when the news flow stops doing the work.
