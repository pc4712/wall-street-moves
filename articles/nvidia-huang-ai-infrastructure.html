Jensen Huang and NVIDIA: Why AI Infrastructure Still Matters

Eleven days after resigning, Jensen Huang is back as NVIDIA chief executive. The board reversed itself, the stock jumped more than 31% in one session, and the AI buildout is back at the center of the market.

By Wall Street Moves · November 24, 2026 · Last updated: November 24, 2026

On Nov. 23, 2026, NVIDIA announced that its board had voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive officer, and director, effective immediately. The decision came 11 days after the company announced Huang's resignation on Nov. 12 — the board's acceptance was dated Nov. 11 in its filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission — and less than a week after the board said on the company's Nov. 17 earnings call that it had no plan to bring him back.

The market reaction was immediate. NVIDIA shares closed at about $165 on Nov. 23, up just over 31% in a single session — the stock's largest one-day gain since May 9, 2003, according to Dow Jones Market Data. The company added roughly $950 billion in market value in one day — the largest single-day gain on record for any U.S. company — ending the session as the second-largest company in the world by market value, behind Apple, after ranking fourth at the close on Nov. 20.

Eleven days that shook the board

Huang resigned on Nov. 12 after reports of extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom U.S. officials suspected of acting on behalf of Chinese intelligence, according to the New York Times. The Times reported on Oct. 29, 2026, that U.S. intelligence officials were concerned she was acting on behalf of Chinese intelligence and had a close relationship with Huang; on Nov. 22, it reported she had been in contact with an official from China's Ministry of State Security for more than a year during the affair. China has denied the allegations, and no finding that she was a spy — or that Huang knew of any intelligence activity — has been established. The Times reported on Nov. 8 that Huang told Nvidia staff he had been unfaithful to his wife, and on Nov. 11 that he had also had an affair with Kimberly Powell, a Nvidia vice president of health care; neither Huang nor Powell confirmed that report. Reuters reported on Nov. 12 that he had also had an affair with another, unnamed Nvidia employee. Chief financial officer Colette Kress became interim chief executive, and Harvey C. Jones was named the first chair in the company's history. The board said it would search inside and outside the company for a successor.

On Nov. 23, the board changed course. In its statement, NVIDIA said the board acted after "a series of discussions and meetings" with Huang and "input from senior levels of the United States government," and that "based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk." That is the board's assertion, not an independent national-security finding. President Donald Trump told CNBC on Nov. 23 that he had spoken with the board and urged the reinstatement, saying he had "convinced them." In a video published by CNBC, Huang told employees, "I'm going to be a better CEO the second time around," as reported by USA TODAY.

Why the market cared so much

Investors were pricing more than a CEO: the founder's relationships with suppliers, major AI customers, and technology partners at the start of a new product cycle — the Vera Rubin NVL72 platform, which NVIDIA said has shipped. Dan Ives, partner and senior managing director at Yorkville Ives, wrote on Nov. 23 that the board reversed itself "because the alternative, a founder-less Nvidia at the start of the Rubin cycle with a CEO search running into next year, was the bigger risk to shareholders."

"At the end of the day despite this Jensen saga the Board knew it had no choice but to bring him back and for the U.S. technology sector this was the move it had to make." — Dan Ives, Yorkville Ives, Nov. 23, 2026

Ives put the stakes in plain terms: "Nvidia is a $300 stock with Jensen and a $225 stock without him" — a valuation he had first set out around Nov. 12–13. He kept his $225 target and Outperform rating, citing the board committee's investigation, government inquiries, unresolved export questions and the board's reversal.

The business never stopped

While the leadership crisis played out, NVIDIA's business kept growing. For the fiscal third quarter ended Oct. 25, 2026, the company reported revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier and above the roughly $109.1 billion analysts expected. Data center revenue reached $107.6 billion, up 110% from a year earlier. Non-GAAP earnings per share were $2.68, against a consensus estimate of about $2.47, and GAAP net income was $65.3 billion (non-GAAP $64.9 billion).

Guidance was strong as well. NVIDIA forecast fourth-quarter revenue of $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, above the roughly $125.3 billion Wall Street expected (FactSet), and interim chief executive Colette Kress raised the fiscal 2028 revenue growth forecast to 75%, from 70%.

Analysts turned positive — with caveats

Bernstein analyst Stacy Rasgon upgraded NVIDIA to Outperform from Market Perform on Nov. 23 and kept his $200 price target. Rasgon had downgraded the stock and cut the target from $400 to $200 on Nov. 12, the day Huang resigned, saying Huang's exit was the primary reason for the downgrade. With Huang back, Rasgon wrote, the firm was upgrading on continued positive AI buildout fundamentals. Four Wall Street firms — Bernstein, Melius, Oppenheimer and StoneX — raised NVIDIA to the equivalent of buy ratings from neutral on Nov. 23.

Rasgon was blunt about the board's handling of the episode, calling the on-again, off-again response "frankly, clown-like," and warned that questions about leadership judgment and corporate governance remain unresolved. He also said the episode revived attention to key-man risk — the company's deep reliance on one person — telling CNBC, "We all knew that there was key man risk here. We all chose … to just ignore it."

What is not settled

Huang's return removes the largest overhang on the stock, in Ives's phrase, but it leaves the others in place. The Wall Street Journal reported on Nov. 17, 2026, citing people familiar with the matter, that the SEC had opened a civil investigation into NVIDIA, including whether the company's disclosures misled investors; the Journal said the full scope could not be determined. Reuters reported on Nov. 20, citing two people briefed on the matter, that SEC enforcement staff were examining potential accounting issues, and that Reuters could not determine when the inquiry began or exactly which accounting issues were under review. Neither report states a finding of wrongdoing, and an investigation is not a finding of wrongdoing.

Other inquiries are also open. California Attorney General Rob Bonta said on Nov. 19 that his office had served an investigative subpoena on NVIDIA, to investigate whether advanced chips or confidential information reached China and whether NVIDIA misled investors and the public about that risk, according to the California Department of Justice and Reuters. Reuters reported on Oct. 28, 2026, citing two people familiar with the matter, that the Justice Department had made inquiries about NVIDIA's $12.93 billion deal to buy Hugging Face and its effect on AI competition, as part of a broader, years-long antitrust investigation; Reuters noted the inquiry may not result in any action. Huang is scheduled for a closed-door, transcribed interview with three House committees on Dec. 1, and a public Senate Banking Committee hearing on Dec. 3. In its Nov. 23 statement, NVIDIA said its board had formed a committee of independent directors to investigate whether foreign intelligence efforts targeted Huang, and that the company is searching for a chief compliance officer.

Why AI infrastructure still matters

The episode tested whether the AI infrastructure story depends on one person, or on demand that exists regardless of who runs NVIDIA. The demand side answered first. The four largest U.S. cloud companies spent a combined $208.7 billion on capital projects in the third quarter of 2026, up 84% from a year earlier, with 2026 guidance totaling roughly $720 billion to $740 billion. NVIDIA's data center revenue, up 110% in the third quarter, is the other side of that same spending.

That is why the reinstatement moved the whole market. The Nasdaq Composite rose 6.51% on Nov. 23, after opening 7.3% higher — the biggest opening gain on record, based on market-opening pricing data back to 1991 — and the S&P 500 rose 4.73%. Chips led: Micron gained 19.04%, SK Hynix gained 20.00%, and AMD gained 6.85%.

The leadership question is settled for now. The governance, regulatory, and export questions are not, and the SEC inquiry has no published timetable. What Nov. 23 established is narrower, and still important: demand for AI infrastructure did not pause while NVIDIA's board argued, and the market paid up the moment the leadership was back in place.
